How to Find the Original Creator of a Pinterest Pin

Guide Published: November 2026 6 min read By Manikant Yadav

A photo gets posted on a blog, someone pins it, a thousand people save that pin, and a year later nobody remembers where it came from. If you want to use a pin anywhere beyond your own boards, or just want to follow the person who made something you love, you need to find who actually created it. The account you see on the pin often isn't them.

Start with the pin itself

Open the pin on its own page (tap it, don't just look at it in the grid). Three things there are worth checking:

  • The link. Many pins point to a website: a recipe blog, a shop, a portfolio. On the pin page that shows up as a link or a "Visit" button, and the domain name is often visible. If the site belongs to a person or small business, it's usually the source.
  • The account name. Click it and look at their other pins. A creator's profile is full of the same style of work, often with their own website in the bio. An account that saves everything from everywhere is a curator, not the creator.
  • The title and description. Creators often write "by", "photo by" or an @handle. Re-pinners usually don't bother.

If the link goes nowhere, to a spammy page, or to a different image entirely, don't trust it. Move on to a reverse search.

Reverse image search (works for most images)

A reverse image search finds other places the same picture appears online. The trick is to search with a good copy. A tiny thumbnail matches badly, so save the full-size image first with the image downloader, then try these:

1

Google Lens

In Chrome, right-click the image and choose Search image with Google, or upload the file at lens.google.com. Scroll to Exact matches; the "visually similar" results above are mostly noise.

2

TinEye

Upload the image at tineye.com and sort by Oldest. The earliest copy it knows about is a strong hint, though not proof, of the original.

3

Bing Visual Search

Bing sometimes finds pages that Google misses, especially shops and smaller blogs. It's worth thirty seconds when the first two come up empty.

Look for a result that has the image at the largest size, the earliest date, and a person's name attached: a photographer's portfolio, an artist's shop, a blog post with the step-by-step photos around it. Those are much stronger signs than one more Pinterest board or an aggregator site.

Videos: look for the watermark

Reverse image search doesn't work well on video, but videos usually leave a better clue. Many Pinterest videos were first posted on TikTok, Instagram Reels or YouTube Shorts, and TikTok's download stamps the creator's @username right on the video. Watch the whole clip, including the last second, for a handle or logo.

If there's no watermark, pause on a distinctive frame (a face, a finished dish, a room), take a screenshot and reverse-search that. If the video has text on screen, searching that exact phrase on TikTok or Instagram can also turn up the original post.

Crediting and asking permission

Once you've found the creator, what you do next depends on how you want to use their work.

What you want to doWhat's expected
Keep it on your own Pinterest boardNothing; that's what pinning is for. Saving the pin from the creator's own account or site helps them.
Save it privately for reference or a mood boardNothing, but keep a note of who made it.
Share it in a blog post or on social mediaAsk first. If they agree, credit them by name with a link.
Use it in an ad, a product or anything paidWritten permission or a licence, every time.

Asking doesn't have to be awkward. Something like this works:

Hi, I came across your [photo/video] of [subject] on Pinterest and love it. I'd like to share it on [where] with credit to you and a link to [your site/profile]. Would that be OK? Happy to use whatever credit line you prefer.

Being specific about where the work will appear and how you'll credit it makes a yes more likely. If they say no or don't reply, don't use it. "I credited them" isn't permission, as our copyright guide explains.

Frequently asked questions

Is the account that pinned something the person who made it?

Often not. Pinterest is built on saving other people's things. Check the link and run a reverse search before assuming.

What if I can't find the creator at all?

Then keep it for personal use only. Content with an unknown owner still has an owner, and "I couldn't find them" doesn't make it free to republish.

Someone is using my work on Pinterest without credit. What can I do?

Pinterest has a copyright report form for pins that use your work. If KlickPint is involved in a way you object to, see our DMCA page or contact us.

Tip: When you find the real creator, re-save the pin from their own profile or website and delete the uncredited copy from your board. It's the easiest way to send them traffic.
