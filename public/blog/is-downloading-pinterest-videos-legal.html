Is It Legal to Download Pinterest Videos? A Plain-English Guide

Guide Updated: November 2026 5 min read
This is general information, not legal advice. Copyright law varies by country, and if you're planning something commercial with downloaded content, a quick conversation with an actual lawyer is worth it.

Short answer: downloading a Pinterest video for your own personal, offline viewing is not something anyone chases people over. What you do with it afterward is what matters.

Who Owns a Pinterest Video?

Pinterest doesn't own the videos on its platform. The person or brand who uploaded it does, the same way a photographer owns their photos even after posting them to Instagram. Pinterest's own Terms of Service are explicit about this: creators keep their copyright, and Pinterest is granted a license to display and distribute the content on its platform, not ownership of it.

That means what matters is whether the person who made this video allows it, not whether Pinterest does. Pinterest's Terms mostly govern your relationship with Pinterest the platform; they don't erase the creator's underlying copyright.

What's Fine

  • Downloading your own content. If you made the video, downloading it back is just retrieving your own file, which is often necessary because Pinterest doesn't offer a built-in "export my videos" button.
  • Personal, offline viewing. Saving a recipe video, a tutorial, or something you want to watch without an internet connection later, purely for yourself, is low-risk in practice. Creators and platforms don't go after people for private viewing copies. Whether it's technically allowed depends on your country's private-copy rules (in the US, it can fall under fair use).
  • Archiving with credit intact. Keeping a private copy for reference, as long as it stays private and isn't redistributed, carries no real risk.

Where It Gets Risky

  • Reposting without permission. Downloading someone's video and re-uploading it to your own Instagram, TikTok, or YouTube, even with a caption crediting them, is technically still distributing their copyrighted work without a license. Some creators are fine with it if tagged; others actively file takedowns. There's no blanket rule that "crediting the source" makes it legal.
  • Commercial use. Using a downloaded video in an ad, a product page, a paid course, or anywhere you're making money from someone else's footage requires explicit permission or a license, full stop.
  • Bulk scraping. Downloading at scale (hundreds or thousands of videos) moves from "personal use" into territory that looks a lot more like unauthorized redistribution of a dataset, which is a different (and much less defensible) legal position.
  • Claiming it as your own. Removing or hiding the original creator's identity and presenting the work as yours is the one thing that reliably crosses from a gray area into a clear violation, regardless of jurisdiction.

What About DMCA?

The Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) is the US legal framework that lets a copyright holder request removal of their content from a site that's hosting or facilitating access to it without permission. In practice, this means: if you download and then republish someone's video, the original creator can file a DMCA takedown against wherever you republished it, not against Pinterest, and not against the tool you used to download it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can Pinterest ban my account for downloading videos?

Using a third-party downloader doesn't touch your Pinterest account at all. You're not logged in anywhere, and no automated action happens on Pinterest's platform under your name. There's nothing for Pinterest to detect or ban.

Can I repost a downloaded video on Instagram or TikTok?

Only if you own the video or have the creator's explicit permission. Reposting someone else's work without credit or consent is a copyright issue regardless of which platform it ends up on, and can get the repost taken down or your account flagged.

What if I'm downloading my own pin?

If you created and own the video, downloading your own content is fine. You're just retrieving a file that's rightfully yours, often because Pinterest itself gives creators no built-in export option.

Is it different for videos that are already "public"?

No. "Public" on Pinterest means anyone can view it without logging in. It says nothing about your right to copy, redistribute, or monetize it. Public visibility and public-domain copyright status are two completely different things.

Rule of thumb: Ask yourself whether you'd need the creator's permission to use their footage in this context if the roles were reversed. Personal viewing rarely needs asking; reposting or monetizing almost always does.
